# Literature Review

## Introduction and review method

This chapter reviews the work on which the thesis builds and against which it must be positioned. It is organised around the questions the research raises. How is threat intelligence used in practice, and what limits its value (Section 2.2)? What does good detection content look like, and how is its quality assessed (Section 2.3)? What can be extracted automatically from threat reports (Section 2.4)? What has been done to generate detection rules with language models (Section 2.5)? How reliable and secure are LLM components in such pipelines (Section 2.6)? And what is known about the human side, namely oversight, automation bias and the measurement of effort (Section 2.7)? What does the economics literature offer for judging cost-effectiveness, and what does "open source" mean for a pipeline that includes a language model (Section 2.8)? Section 2.9 synthesises the findings into the research gap that Chapter 1 stated.

**Method.** The review is a structured narrative review, not a systematic review conforming to a protocol such as PRISMA. Searches were run on 2 October 2026 with a general web-search service, which returns publisher pages, preprint servers, proceedings and code repositories; no citation-database export was used. Searches were organised by theme (Table 2.1). Central papers were then searched by title and author to confirm bibliographic details, and citation chasing from the closest works identified further sources. Peer-reviewed venues and credible preprints from 2017 to 2026 were included where they bore directly on one of the themes; practitioner sources were included only where they define widely used frameworks or formats, and are labelled as such. Marketing material, vendor blogs without a method, and sources that could not be matched to an identifiable author or publisher were excluded.

## Cyber threat intelligence: from sharing to operational use

**Concepts and standards.** Threat intelligence is exchanged in structured forms, of which STIX is the OASIS standard for representing it [@oasis2021stix], and under organisational guidance on how to share it responsibly [@johnson2016nist]. Surveys of technical threat intelligence and of CTI sharing describe the standards, analytics and sharing approaches, and open problems of the field [@tounsi2018survey; @wagner2019sharing]. This body of work establishes *how* intelligence is represented and exchanged. It is largely silent on how much effort is needed to act on it.

**Quality, overlap and value.** Empirical studies show that the intelligence supply is heterogeneous. Comparative analyses of threat intelligence sources examine what different providers contribute and how they relate to each other [@li2019tea]. The assessment of two leading commercial providers found almost no overlap between their indicators, nor with four large open feeds, with an average overlap of 2.5% to 4.0% for 22 threat actors tracked by both, and overlapping indicators appearing in the other vendor's feed with an average delay of about a month [@bouwman2020cup]. A multi-month evaluation of 24 open-source feeds found significant variation in quality, as well as biases towards certain countries and the risk of collateral damage when blocking listed IP addresses [@griffioen2020quality]. For this thesis, two points follow. Indicator feeds cannot be treated as ground truth, so indicators must be validated, filtered and given an expiry rather than deployed wholesale. And the interviews in the commercial-intelligence study found that customers value analyst time rather than detection coverage alone [@bouwman2020cup], which makes analyst effort, and not only detection accuracy, the right outcome to measure.

**Indicators versus behaviour.** The Pyramid of Pain ranks indicator types by how much it hurts an adversary to have them detected: hash values and similar low-level indicators are trivial to change, whereas detecting TTPs forces the adversary to change behaviour [@bianco2013pyramid]. ATT&CK supplies a shared vocabulary for that behaviour [@strom2018attack]. The consequence for design is that indicator detections and behaviour detections are different artefacts with different lifetimes and different false-positive profiles. The IOC bundle and CDB list used for indicators and the Sigma rule used for behaviours in this thesis follow this distinction. The half-lives used in the recency score (Chapter 4) are design parameters of this thesis, not findings from the literature, and are subject to sensitivity analysis.

## Detection engineering and the quality of detection content

**Practice and formats.** Detection engineering is documented largely through practitioner frameworks rather than academic theory. The Alerting and Detection Strategy framework asks authors to record a detection's goal, categorisation, strategy abstract, technical context, blind spots and assumptions, false positives, validation, priority and response [@palantir2017ads]. Sigma provides the portable rule format, describing log events in YAML independently of the SIEM that will run the rule [@sigmahq2024spec]. Both inform ATIDEP's data model: the use-case fields it requires, the declaration of engineering assumptions, and the mandatory false-positive hypotheses.

**Conversion to a target platform.** Portable rules must be converted. Wazuh is a free and open-source XDR and SIEM platform [@wazuh2026overview] whose rules are XML elements combined conjunctively, with constant-database lists for exact-match lookups [@wazuh2026docs]. Two community converters from Sigma to Wazuh document the difficulty. One compiles the SigmaHQ corpus at a reported 96% rate, states that Wazuh rules cannot natively hold disjunctions so that OR logic becomes sibling rules, and refuses what it cannot translate faithfully [@zshguy2026tyrian]. The other warns that some logic conversion remains broken because of the complexity of mapping Sigma OR logic into Wazuh, lists unsupported features (timeframes, `near` logic, aggregation), and is no longer updated in its Python form [@theflakes2024sigmatowazuh]. These projects are software, not peer-reviewed studies, but they establish the engineering constraints on which the converter in this thesis is designed: an explicit supported subset, expansion of OR logic, and refusal rather than approximation.

**Assessing the quality of rules.** Work on the quality of detection content is recent and, in part, not yet peer reviewed. A static quality framework for Sigma rules, implemented in a tool called sigmalint and applied to the 3,132 rules of the public SigmaHQ corpus, separates *validity*, a binary conformance gate, from *quality*, a weighted score across six dimensions, and tests its checks with a seeded-defect benchmark of nine mutation operators [@tyagi2026sigmalint]. This is the same separation that ATIDEP makes between hard gates and a ranking score, and the same seeded-defect idea that ATIDEP applies to its own gates (Chapter 3); the difference is that ATIDEP applies the idea to a generation pipeline's gates rather than to a rule corpus. A study of nine years of SigmaHQ history (8,234 logic-changing revisions) found that exclusions are added about 5.4 times for every time one is withdrawn, and that 86.7% of exclusions are still in force three years after they are added [@dhananjeyan2026exclusion]. For a platform that proposes tuning exclusions automatically, this is a warning: false-positive suppression tends to ratchet towards narrower rules and rarely relaxes, so exclusion proposals need review, expiry and a retention check on true positives, which is why the feedback hypothesis (H6) requires true-positive retention as well as false-positive reduction.

A third result shows why validity is not enough. A deterministic pipeline that turns attack-simulation findings into starter Sigma rules produced rules that all parsed and converted to two query languages (17 of 17), but when replayed through a live SIEM they fired on only 30% of one held-out attack subset and 14% of another, at 7.7% false positives on a benign baseline [@maiorano2026probe]. Whatever the merits of that particular system, the lesson generalises: rules can be syntactically perfect and operationally weak, so validation must include tests against events, and quality must be judged on detection behaviour, not on conformance alone.

**The operational setting.** Detection quality matters because analysts pay for poor content. In interviews and a survey of SOC practitioners, analysts confirmed high false-positive rates requiring manual validation, attributed most of them to benign triggers, and elicited five properties of good alarms: reliable, explainable, analytical, contextual and transferable [@alahmadi2022false]. These properties are used in this thesis as design requirements for ATIDEP's outputs (evidence-linked, explained, tested, with triage guidance), and the distinction between a false positive and a *benign true positive* is carried into the analyst-disposition categories.

## Extracting attack behaviour from threat reports

A long line of work extracts structured knowledge from threat-report text. TTPDrill used a threat-action ontology with natural-language processing and information retrieval to extract threat actions and map them to ATT&CK techniques and tactics, reporting more than 82% precision and recall on various measures for randomly selected vendor reports [@husari2017ttpdrill]. rcATT evaluated several classification approaches for retrieving tactics and techniques from unstructured reports and released a tool [@legoy2020rcatt]. LADDER extracts text-based attack patterns at scale and maps them to ATT&CK [@alam2023ladder]. EXTRACTOR extracts concise attack behaviours as provenance graphs from unstructured text, evaluated on real incident reports and on DARPA adversarial engagements across Windows, Linux and FreeBSD [@satvat2021extractor].

LLMs changed the approach. The aCTIon pipelines use a large language model (GPT-3.5) for structured CTI extraction and are evaluated on a new benchmark of 204 public reports with structured STIX annotations curated by three independent groups of analysts, reporting F1 improvements of 10 to 50 percentage points over previous work across tasks [@siracusano2023action]. CTIBench, a NeurIPS 2024 benchmark, was motivated by persistent concerns about the reliability, accuracy and hallucinations of LLMs in CTI, and evaluates them on analyst-centric tasks such as ATT&CK mapping, vulnerability severity and attribution [@alam2024ctibench]. A systematic review of 185 papers on LLMs in cyber security shows how broad the application area has become, spanning vulnerability detection, malware analysis and intrusion detection [@xu2024llmsecurity].

Two observations carry forward. First, this line of work stops at structured *knowledge*: techniques, patterns, graphs. As described in the sources consulted, none of these systems produces a tested, deployable detection rule, so the remaining steps (telemetry, rule logic, validation, conversion, approval) stay manual. Second, extraction accuracy is imperfect even for the best systems, and the concern about hallucination is explicit in the benchmark literature. Downstream stages must therefore tolerate extraction errors, which motivates ATIDEP's design: every extracted claim must be a verbatim quotation of the source, verified by substring match, and claims that fail verification are excluded from scoring and rule generation and counted as unsupported.

## Large language models for detection-rule generation

Four recent works are closest to this thesis.

**LLMCloudHunter** uses LLMs to generate generic-signature detection rule candidates from textual and visual open-source CTI about cloud environments. It reports 92% precision and 98% recall for extracting API calls made by threat actors, 99% precision and 98% recall for indicators, and that 99.18% of generated rule candidates compiled into Splunk queries [@schwartz2024llmcloudhunter].

**SIGMERGE** generates Sigma rules from the text of cyber threat reports by constructing a semantic intermediate layer. A fine-tuned domain-specific LLM extracts ATT&CK TTPs and commands; an attack-description module uses preference-optimisation tuning with closed-loop self-validation; and a rule-generation module uses a parameter-optimised retrieval algorithm. It is evaluated with 23 metrics against 16 baselines and 13 LLMs, with 10 case studies integrated with real security systems, and contributed four new rules accepted into the official Sigma repository [@cai2026sigmerge].

**AutoSigma** performs contextual analysis, template-driven retrieval of existing Sigma rules using a structured knowledge base, and generative refinement, and uses an LLM-as-a-judge mechanism to validate rules iteratively. It is evaluated on real-world APT reports and security blogs for rule validity, relevance (BERTScore), ATT&CK coverage and robustness to input quality, and the cloud-based variant is reported to achieve 100% rule validity [@ghaffarzadegan2026autosigma].

**CTI-REALM** is a benchmark from Microsoft Security AI in which an agent reads CTI reports, explores telemetry, iterates on KQL queries and submits Sigma rules and KQL detection logic, using tools for report retrieval, schema inspection, KQL execution, ATT&CK lookup and Sigma search, scored against ground truth. Sixteen model configurations were evaluated, with the best overall reward at 0.637 [@chakraborty2026ctirealm]. Secondary descriptions report that tasks requiring correlation across several log sources were markedly harder.

These works are significant. Together they show that LLM-based rule generation from reports is feasible, that retrieval of existing rules and intermediate representations improve it, and that realistic evaluation environments are now available. They are also strong on the dimensions they chose to measure: validity, relevance, coverage, compilation, and, in CTI-REALM, execution against telemetry. Some of them include a validation-and-refinement loop of their own (self-validation in SIGMERGE, LLM-as-a-judge in AutoSigma), and baseline comparisons are standard (SIGMERGE against 16 baselines and 13 LLMs; AutoSigma against alternative solutions and LLMs). The thesis builds on the idea of a validate-and-revise loop but differs in what drives it: ATIDEP's loop is driven by deterministic validators and tests, not by a model judging a model, for reasons given in Section 2.6.

Two further pieces of context complete the picture. Reviews of LLMs in security [@xu2024llmsecurity] and benchmarks of LLMs in CTI [@alam2024ctibench] give the broader empirical background. And the community converters discussed in Section 2.3 [@zshguy2026tyrian; @theflakes2024sigmatowazuh] show that the last step, getting a rule into an open-source SIEM faithfully, is a nontrivial engineering problem of its own, which the generation literature mostly treats as out of scope or reduces to compilation into a commercial query language.

## Reliability and security of LLM components

**Hallucination.** Hallucination, generated content that is fluent but unsupported by the source, is a well-surveyed property of natural language generation [@ji2023hallucination]. In detection engineering its forms are specific: invented field names, conditions that appear nowhere in the report, and wrong technique mappings. The CTI benchmark literature names it as a motivating concern [@alam2024ctibench]. The design response in this thesis is to constrain what the model may say (allowed field lists, verbatim quotations) and to verify mechanically what it did say.

**Prompt injection.** Perez and Ribeiro described goal hijacking and prompt leaking against instruction-following models [@perez2022ignore]. Greshake and colleagues introduced indirect prompt injection, in which instructions placed in content that an LLM-integrated application retrieves are enough to compromise it, and demonstrated attacks including data theft and the manipulation of application behaviour [@greshake2023indirect]. The OWASP guidance for LLM applications lists prompt injection first and recommends restricting the model's access to necessary operations, requiring human approval for privileged actions, limiting the influence of untrusted content, and establishing trust boundaries [@owasp2023llmtop10]. A CTI pipeline is a textbook exposure: it ingests untrusted text from the open internet and produces content that may later be executed in a security system. The controls in this thesis (tool-less agents, sanitisation of hidden content, schema-validated output, verbatim-quote verification, a benign-indicator allowlist, deterministic overrides and hash-bound approval) follow these recommendations, and are tested by a prompt-injection report set (Chapter 3).

**Refinement loops.** Iterative refinement improves LLM output. Self-Refine has one model generate, critique and revise its own output and reports an average improvement of about 20% absolute across seven tasks [@madaan2023selfrefine]. Self-Debugging teaches a model to debug generated programs using execution feedback and explanations, with gains of up to 12% on some code-generation benchmarks and 9% on the hardest Spider problems [@chen2024selfdebug]. The second is closer to ATIDEP's repair loop, because the feedback comes from an external check, not from the model's own opinion. In ATIDEP the external check is a set of deterministic validators whose structured defect reports are the only feedback the model receives.

**LLMs as judges.** Strong LLM judges can match human preferences, but they show position, verbosity and self-enhancement biases and limited reasoning ability [@zheng2023judge]. For a safety gate, these biases are disqualifying: a gate that a model can talk its way past is not a gate. ATIDEP therefore does not use an LLM as the arbiter of whether a rule may proceed, and quality is judged by blinded human raters and by behaviour on held-out events. This is a design difference from systems that use LLM-based judging inside the generation loop [@ghaffarzadegan2026autosigma], not a criticism of their evaluation.

**Training-data contamination.** Evaluating a model on material it has seen during training inflates results, and the extent of the problem is difficult to measure [@sainz2023contamination]. Sigma rules and threat reports are public and widely scraped, so a model may have memorised rules for well-known techniques. The thesis responds with reports published after the model's training cutoff and a similarity check of generated rules against a pinned SigmaHQ commit.

**Workflows and agents.** Industry guidance distinguishes workflows, in which LLMs and tools are orchestrated through predefined code paths, from agents, in which the LLM directs its own process, and advises starting simple and adding agency only when the flexibility is needed [@schluntz2024effective]. ATIDEP is predominantly a workflow with bounded agentic components: the repair loop gives the model limited autonomy over how it revises a rule, but the number of iterations, the information it receives and the exit conditions are fixed by code. The thesis uses "agent" only for these LLM-backed components.

## Humans in the loop

Approval by a human is the final control in ATIDEP, and the literature on automation bias says it cannot be assumed to be effective. A review of empirical studies found that complacency and automation bias occur in both naive and expert participants, cannot be overcome by simple practice, and cannot be prevented by training or instructions [@parasuraman2010complacency]. A systematic review of 74 studies of automation bias, framed around clinical decision support, found that user factors, system design characteristics and attitudes all influence over-reliance, and that decision aids can introduce new errors that users fail to recognise [@goddard2012automation]. These findings argue against treating the human reviewer as a guarantee. ATIDEP therefore makes the reviewer's job explicit and checkable: the interface shows verified evidence quotations, the results of every gate and the declared assumptions; approval is bound to the content hash; and the experiment re-reviews a sample of approvals for agreement. The researcher being both author and approver in the laboratory is a limitation that the thesis states.

On measurement, the same evidence supports an efficiency outcome based on analyst active time [@bouwman2020cup] and an evaluation of quality that does not depend on the system's own scoring. Where the claim is that automation does *not* make rules worse, a non-significant difference does not show equivalence; the appropriate frame is a pre-specified equivalence or non-inferiority margin tested with two one-sided tests [@schuirmann1987tost; @lakens2017equivalence]. The overall research design follows the design-science tradition of building an artefact and evaluating it rigorously [@hevner2004design; @peffers2007dsrm].

## Cost, economic evaluation and open-source claims

**Security as an economic decision.** Anderson argued that information insecurity is as much a matter of incentives as of technology, and explained many failures through concepts such as asymmetric information, moral hazard and externalities [@anderson2001hard]. Gordon and Loeb's model of security investment shows how an organisation can reason about how much to spend; in it, the optimal investment does not exceed about 37% (1/e) of the expected loss from a breach [@gordon2002economics]. These works justify treating cost as a first-class dimension of a security design. They do not cost a detection-engineering workflow, and the thesis uses them for framing only.

**Total cost of ownership.** Total cost of ownership (TCO) is a concept from purchasing and supply-chain management that looks beyond the purchase price when comparing alternatives, and its models have been studied in practice [@ellram1998tco; @ferrin2002tco]. It is the reason the thesis separates development, infrastructure, inference, operation and maintenance costs, and reports a build view and an adopt view (Chapter 8).

**Analyst time and human cost.** Practitioners using commercial CTI were found to optimise for analyst time [@bouwman2020cup], which supports treating analyst labour as the principal cost driver and measuring it directly. An anthropological study of a corporate SOC, conducted over six months, described analyst burnout as a human-capital management problem arising from the interaction of human, technical and managerial factors [@sundaramurthy2015burnout]. The implication is that effort savings may have value beyond wages. The thesis does not quantify this and records it in the hidden-cost register instead.

**Claims about open-source cost.** Vendor and practitioner material asserts large total-cost differences between open-source and commercial SIEMs, and repeats the caution that "free" software still incurs infrastructure, storage, support and skilled-labour costs. These sources were reviewed but excluded under the criteria in Section 2.1: they are vendor or blog material without a stated method, and some are published by sellers of cost calculators. The thesis therefore relies on no external estimate of open-source versus commercial cost. It measures its own workflow and states its assumptions.

**What "open source" means.** The Open Source Definition sets out ten criteria for open-source software, including free redistribution, availability of source code, and no discrimination against persons, groups or fields of endeavour [@osi2007osd]. For AI systems, the Open Source AI Definition 1.0 requires, in addition to OSI-approved code and shared parameters, sufficiently detailed information about the training data, so that releasing weights alone, commonly called "open weights", is not enough [@osi2024osaid]. This matters because the platform includes a language model. A "fully open-source" claim is accurate for the software stack, but a model released only as open weights does not meet the OSI's definition of open-source AI. The thesis therefore audits the licence of every component, classifies the model, and states the claim precisely.

**Cost-aware use of LLMs.** Language-model prices differ greatly. FrugalGPT notes that the fees for querying different LLM APIs can differ by two orders of magnitude, and reports that strategies such as cascading cheaper models before expensive ones can reduce inference cost by up to 98% while matching the best single model's performance on the tasks studied [@chen2024frugalgpt]. RouteLLM trains routers that choose between a stronger and a weaker model, and reports cost reductions of more than a factor of two in some cases without loss of response quality [@ong2025routellm]. Both treat cost and quality as a trade-off to be managed. ATIDEP's design is simpler: local-first inference, with cloud inference as an explicit opt-in under a hard daily cap. Price-aware routing is a natural extension and is listed as future work.

**Implications.** Three points carry forward. The cost analysis should be TCO-style, with explicit assumptions and a build and an adopt view. Analyst time is expected to dominate, and it is a direct output of the efficiency experiment, so the cost verdict depends largely on that result and on the documented hourly rates. And the open-source claim needs a licence audit that includes the model.

Five conclusions follow, each bounded by the limits stated in Section 2.1.

1. **Fail-closed governance.** The generation systems report strong validity and relevance results, and some include model-driven validation loops, but the sources consulted do not describe a set of hard pass/fail gates that blocks a rule from deployment independently of any score, nor an approval bound to the exact content of the rule. The static-quality work does separate a binary validity gate from a weighted score [@tyagi2026sigmalint], supporting the design principle, but it assesses existing rules and does not govern generation.
2. **An open-source SIEM target with explicit refusal.** The generation literature produces Sigma or targets commercial query languages (Splunk, KQL), and the sources consulted do not address delivery to an open-source SIEM such as Wazuh. The community converters for Wazuh document the engineering constraints [@zshguy2026tyrian; @theflakes2024sigmatowazuh], but the sources consulted do not combine such a converter with an LLM generation pipeline, a Sigma-level matcher, and a measured fidelity check against the target's own rule tester.
3. **Analyst effort.** Analyst time is what practitioners value [@bouwman2020cup], yet the consulted summaries of the rule-generation works do not report a comparison of analyst active time against a manual workflow under a design that controls order and learning effects. Quality is generally measured by automatic metrics and, at most, by comparison with reference rules, not by blinded raters and held-out events, and the non-inferiority framing is absent.
4. **Testing the controls.** Injection and hallucination are well characterised in general [@greshake2023indirect; @ji2023hallucination], and seeded-defect testing exists for linters [@tyagi2026sigmalint], but the consulted sources do not report applying a seeded-defect set and a prompt-injection report set to the gates of a CTI-to-rule pipeline and measuring how many defective rules escape.
5. **Cost-effectiveness.** The consulted summaries of the generation works report quality and compilation measures but, in the sources consulted, no unit cost per accepted rule and no break-even analysis. The security-economics literature supplies frameworks for thinking about security spending [@gordon2002economics; @anderson2001hard] rather than costings of detection-engineering workflows, and the cost claims found for open-source SIEMs rest on vendor material that cannot serve as evidence.

Baseline comparison is common in this literature, so that is not claimed as a gap. The contribution of the single-prompt condition here is different: it receives the same information as the pipeline, and the ablations remove the validators and the repair loop, so that differences can be attributed to structure and controls rather than to information access.

**Positioning.** ATIDEP is therefore not presented as a more accurate Sigma generator than SIGMERGE, AutoSigma or LLMCloudHunter. Its contribution is a governed, fail-closed workflow for an open-source target, together with an evaluation that measures what practitioners care about and that tests the controls adversarially. If the full-text check reveals that a prior work already covers any of the five points above, the corresponding contribution in Chapter 1 will be narrowed accordingly.

The gaps map onto the research questions of Chapter 1: fail-closed governance and adversarial testing to RQ3 and RQ5, the target-platform converter to RQ3, analyst effort and quality to the main question and RQ6, tuning to RQ7, and cost-effectiveness to RQ8.

## Summary

The literature shows that threat intelligence is abundant, heterogeneous and costly to operationalise; that behaviour-based detections are more durable than indicators but harder to produce; that extraction of attack behaviour from text is established but imperfect; and that LLM-based generation of Sigma rules has recently become feasible and well benchmarked. It also shows that LLM components bring specific risks (hallucination, injection, judge bias, contamination), that human approval is vulnerable to automation bias, that rule validity does not guarantee detection quality, that cost claims for open-source security tooling rest largely on vendor material that the thesis does not rely on, and that "open source" needs a precise definition when a language model is part of the stack. What it does not yet show, in the sources consulted, is how a fail-closed, human-approved workflow for an open-source SIEM compares with manual and single-prompt work in analyst effort, independently judged quality and resistance to attack. The next chapter sets out the methodology designed to answer that question.
