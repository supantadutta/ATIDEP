# Introduction

## What ATIDEP is

**ATIDEP (Agentic Threat Intelligence and Detection Engineering Platform) is an open-source-oriented, LLM-assisted threat-intelligence-to-detection-engineering pipeline that ingests threat reports, extracts security-relevant information, generates detection content, validates generated rules through deterministic security and quality gates, supports human approval and controlled deployment, and records feedback, analyst effort and resource use from which operational cost is evaluated.**

In practical terms, ATIDEP converts threat intelligence into reviewed and deployable detection content while maintaining traceability and human control. A threat report goes in; what comes out is a Sigma detection rule (for a described behaviour) or an indicator list (for indicators of compromise), each tied to quotations from the report that justify it, each passed through deterministic checks (a rule is also tested against example events), and each approved by a named person before it can be deployed to a laboratory Wazuh manager. Language models do the reading and drafting; deterministic code decides whether anything they produce may proceed.

The platform has five components:

- **C1 Ingest** (deterministic) collects reports from approved feeds, uploaded files (text, HTML, PDF) and allow-listed addresses through a fetcher that refuses private and unexpected destinations, removes hidden and invisible text, stores the original and the sanitised text with checksums, and discards duplicates.
- **C2 Intelligence processing** extracts indicators deterministically (with defanged values restored and well-known benign domains excluded), and uses an Extraction Agent to identify behaviours and entities. Every extracted claim must carry a quotation that is found verbatim in the sanitised report; a claim whose quotation does not verify is never used. Behaviours are mapped to a pinned ATT&CK release, and each item receives a transparent priority score.
- **C3 Detection engineering** decides, with an Opportunity Agent and a deterministic check against the organisation's telemetry catalogue, whether the item supports a defensible detection and of what kind. A Rule Agent drafts a Sigma rule for a behaviour; an indicator builder produces a list for Wazuh's constant-database lookups. A bounded repair loop lets the Rule Agent revise a rule at most twice, using only the structured defect reports of the validators.
- **C4 Validation and governance** (deterministic) runs eleven hard pass/fail gates (G1-G11: syntax and schema, metadata, a Wazuh-compatible Sigma subset and its converter, evidence traceability, field validity, telemetry availability, ATT&CK mapping, positive, negative and benign look-alike tests, and a breadth limit on a benign baseline), and records a human approval that is bound to the SHA-256 of the exact rule content and of its validation report, in a hash-chained audit trail.
- **C5 Deployment and feedback** builds a Wazuh package that can be exported, dry-run or deployed to the laboratory manager through a least-privilege API account, with rollback; collects alerts and analyst dispositions; and lets an Improvement Agent propose changes that return to validation and approval as a new rule version.

Around these components, the platform records analyst active time with a timer, every model call with its token counts and where those counts came from, and machine time, so that the effort and the cost of the workflow can be analysed (Chapters 7 and 8).

## Background and problem context

Cyber threat intelligence (CTI) is information about adversaries, their infrastructure and their methods that defenders can use to anticipate and respond to attacks. Guidance such as the NIST guide to cyber threat information sharing describes how organisations establish sharing relationships, the benefits and difficulties of sharing, and the automated exchange of threat information [@johnson2016nist]. Surveys of the field document how technical threat intelligence and its sharing have grown into an established topic of research and practice [@tounsi2018survey; @wagner2019sharing].

The supply of intelligence is large, but it is not uniform in quality or in agreement. Comparative analyses of threat intelligence data sources have examined how the sources relate to one another [@li2019tea]. An empirical assessment of two leading commercial providers found almost no overlap between their indicators, or with four large open feeds; for 22 threat actors tracked by both vendors, the average overlap was only 2.5% to 4.0% [@bouwman2020cup]. A separate evaluation of 24 open-source feeds over several months found significant variation in their quality [@griffioen2020quality]. The same commercial-intelligence study reports a finding that frames the motivation for this thesis: in 14 interviews with practitioners, customers were found to optimise for analyst time rather than for threat detection as such [@bouwman2020cup]. The practical value of intelligence therefore depends not only on what it contains but on how cheaply a team can turn it into protection.

Turning intelligence into protection is the work of detection engineering. Intelligence arrives in two broad forms. *Indicators of compromise* (IP addresses, domains, file hashes) are easy to match but, as the Pyramid of Pain argues, also easy for an adversary to change, while *tactics, techniques and procedures* (TTPs) are the hardest for an adversary to change and so cause the most disruption when detected [@bianco2013pyramid]. MITRE ATT&CK provides a common taxonomy of adversary behaviour that is used to convey threat intelligence, to test defences, and to improve them [@strom2018attack]. Behaviour-based detections are usually written as rules for a security information and event management (SIEM) or extended detection and response (XDR) platform. Sigma is a generic, open, YAML-based rule format that is compiled to the query language of a particular SIEM, which lets one rule serve many platforms [@sigmahq2024spec]. Practitioner frameworks such as Palantir's Alerting and Detection Strategy framework prescribe what a detection should document, including its goal, technical context, blind spots and assumptions, false positives, and validation [@palantir2017ads].

Cost is the other side of the problem. Security spending competes with other uses of an organisation's money [@gordon2002economics], and incentives and economics shape security outcomes as much as technology does [@anderson2001hard]. For a small team or an academic laboratory, the question is therefore not only whether automating detection engineering works, but what it costs and whether a predominantly open-source workflow can deliver strong, practically useful performance at a substantially lower cost than a comparable paid one. Open source is not the same as zero cost: licence fees may vanish while compute, hardware, electricity, storage, model inference, analyst labour and maintenance remain. This thesis therefore evaluates ATIDEP on two dimensions, technical effectiveness and economic cost-effectiveness, and treats the second as a testable question and not a slogan. It defines in advance what "substantially cheaper" means and how a conclusion will be drawn, builds a cost model whose every price, rate and volume is either taken from a cited source or left explicitly missing, compares the workflow with the manual workflow and with a modelled paid configuration of the same functions, and reports where the conclusion holds and where it does not, in whichever direction the evidence points.

## Problem statement

Turning a threat report into operational detection content is not one task but a chain of them, and each link needs judgement:

1. collecting and reviewing reports from sources whose quality and reliability differ;
2. identifying the behaviours and indicators that are relevant to the organisation, and setting aside what is stale, duplicated, vague or irrelevant;
3. determining what is *observable*: which log source and which fields would reveal the behaviour, and whether the organisation collects them;
4. translating narrative intelligence into detection logic;
5. validating that logic: its syntax, its meaning, and its compatibility with the target platform's event schema and rule language;
6. assessing false-positive risk against ordinary activity in the environment;
7. obtaining analyst review and an accountable approval;
8. deploying the content safely, with the ability to roll it back;
9. monitoring the results and improving the detection.

Done by hand, this chain is slow and depends on experienced people. The downstream cost of poorly engineered rules is well documented: in a qualitative study of security operations centre (SOC) analysts, practitioners confirmed that the tools they use produce high rates of false-positive alarms that require manual validation, although most of these were attributed to benign triggers, that is, true alarms explained by legitimate behaviour in the organisation's environment [@alahmadi2022false]. Detection content that is hard to explain, hard to validate and noisy adds to the burden it is meant to reduce.

Large language models (LLMs) promise to automate parts of this chain, from extracting behaviours out of prose to drafting rules. They also introduce failure modes that matter for security content. Language models can *hallucinate*, producing fluent output that is not supported by the source [@ji2023hallucination]; for a detection rule this can mean an invented field name, a condition the report never described, or a technique mapping that does not hold. Models that read external text can also be manipulated by instructions embedded in that text. Attack techniques such as goal hijacking and prompt leaking were described early [@perez2022ignore], and *indirect* prompt injection extended the threat model to any content that an LLM-integrated application retrieves and reads [@greshake2023indirect]. Threat reports are exactly such content: they are collected from the open internet, so their text cannot be assumed to be trustworthy. Guidance for LLM applications consequently recommends restricting a model's access to what is necessary, requiring human approval for privileged operations, and limiting the influence of untrusted content [@owasp2023llmtop10].

**Why a single prompt is not automatically enough.** The simplest use of an LLM here is to give it the report and ask for a rule. That can produce a plausible rule, but nothing in the arrangement guarantees the properties the chain above requires. A single prompt does not check that each condition is supported by the report rather than by the model's prior knowledge; it does not know which fields and log sources the organisation actually collects; a rule can be valid YAML and valid Sigma and still detect the wrong thing, use a construct the target platform cannot express, or match much of the organisation's normal activity; the report's own text can steer the output; the result differs from one run to the next; and there is no record of who approved what, on what evidence. These are not claims that a single prompt performs badly; they are reasons why its output cannot be trusted *without further controls*. Whether the controls make a measurable difference is an empirical question, and this thesis tests it directly: condition C of the experiment gives the same model the same information in one prompt, with no pipeline and no validators, and compares the result with the full workflow (H5, Chapter 3).

The research problem of this thesis is therefore not whether an LLM can draft a detection rule. It is whether a controlled and auditable workflow, with deterministic validation around bounded LLM steps, can turn heterogeneous threat intelligence into useful, deployable detection content with less analyst effort and without loss of rule quality or operational safety; whether the controls, rather than the language model alone, account for that safety; and what such a workflow costs compared with doing the work by hand and with a comparable paid configuration.

## State of the art and research gap in brief

Chapter 2 reviews the state of the art by theme and compares the closest works in a matrix (Table 2.10); this section states the conclusion that motivates the research questions. Every paper was checked through abstract-level or metadata extracts only, so each statement below is bounded by what those extracts show, and full-text verification is pending (Section 2.1).

Research relevant to ATIDEP falls into four groups. *Extraction* systems, from TTPDrill and rcATT to LADDER, EXTRACTOR, aCTIon and TTPrint, turn reports into structured attack knowledge, the last with evidence located in the text [@husari2017ttpdrill; @legoy2020rcatt; @alam2023ladder; @satvat2021extractor; @siracusano2023action; @cheng2026ttprint]. *Generation* systems draft detection content from intelligence with language models: SIGMERGE and AutoSigma produce Sigma rules, LLMCloudHunter produces rule candidates for cloud logs, FALCON generates network and file signatures (Snort, YARA) from intelligence with built-in validators, Tseng et al. extract indicators and generate matching expressions for a SIEM, GenTI generates intrusion-prevention and YARA rules for unseen attacks, RulePilot creates and converts rules from analysts' natural-language descriptions, and CTI-REALM benchmarks agents on the whole task in a controlled environment [@cai2026sigmerge; @ghaffarzadegan2026autosigma; @schwartz2024llmcloudhunter; @mitra2025falcon; @tseng2024ctiagent; @hadi2026genti; @wang2025rulepilot; @chakraborty2026ctirealm]. *Validation, conversion and quality* work lints Sigma rules, studies rule repositories, and converts Sigma to Wazuh, the latter with a compiler that refuses what it cannot translate [@tyagi2026sigmalint; @dhananjeyan2026exclusion; @zshguy2026tyrian; @theflakes2024sigmatowazuh]. *Governance and operations* work places evidence, provenance and revocable trust decisions around extracted claims, and applies language models to alert triage and query generation in security operations [@valletta2026tracecti; @saju2026autonomoussoc].

Two findings narrow what this thesis can claim. First, generating detection rules from threat intelligence with language models is established, and several systems report it working well; ATIDEP does not claim a better generator. Second, the one study found that generates Wazuh rules with a language model (from an organisation profile rather than from threat intelligence) reports a negative result: on production events, the rule set augmented with the generated rules performed worse than the native rules alone [@schafhauzer2026context]. A positive outcome on this target cannot be presumed.

What the extracts do not show is a single workflow that carries an item from report to an approved, deployed and tuned rule under one set of fail-closed controls, attacks those controls with seeded defects and injected reports, measures analyst effort against a manual workflow under a design that controls order and learning, and evaluates cost against a defined, comparable paid configuration. The reviewed literature therefore indicates limited evidence of an integrated approach that jointly evaluates the technical and the economic side of LLM-assisted detection engineering. ATIDEP's intended contribution lies in that integration, in governance and in the evaluation, not in any single component, several of which have precedents (Section 2.16).

## Aim and objectives

**Aim.** To design, implement, and experimentally evaluate an open-source platform, ATIDEP (Agentic Threat Intelligence and Detection Engineering Platform), that transforms heterogeneous cyber threat intelligence into prioritised, explainable, validated and human-approved detection content, to determine the effect of its workflow and controls on analyst effort, rule quality and safety relative to manual and single-prompt baselines, and to determine from measured quantities and a stated, sourced cost model whether it delivers that performance at a substantially lower cost than a manual workflow and a modelled paid configuration of the same functions.

The aim is pursued through the following objectives.

1. Ingest intelligence from a small set of approved sources (feeds, uploaded files and allowlisted URLs) safely, preserving the original evidence.
2. Extract indicators, behaviours and entities, each linked to a verbatim quotation that is verified against the source text.
3. Prioritise intelligence with a transparent, reproducible scoring model and decide, with deterministic telemetry checks, whether it supports a defensible detection.
4. Generate Sigma rules for behaviours and Wazuh constant-database (CDB) lists for indicators.
5. Validate generated content through hard pass/fail gates and positive, negative and benign look-alike tests, with a bounded automated repair loop.
6. Convert a documented subset of Sigma to Wazuh rules, refusing constructs that cannot be translated faithfully, and test the result at two tiers.
7. Record human approval bound to the exact rule content, with versioning, rollback and a tamper-evident audit trail.
8. Evaluate the workflow against a manual workflow and a single-prompt LLM baseline in a pre-registered, bias-controlled experiment, including seeded-defect and prompt-injection tests of the controls.

## Research questions

The main research question is:

> To what extent does a governed, open-source agentic workflow reduce the analyst effort needed to turn cyber threat intelligence into validated, deployable detection content, without reducing independently judged rule quality or operational safety, how much of the result is attributable to the pipeline's structure and validators rather than to the language model alone; and what does the workflow cost, per unit of detection content and under stated workloads, relative to a manual workflow and to a modelled paid configuration of the same functions?

It is supported by the following questions.

- **RQ1.** How accurately does the platform extract indicators, behaviours and context, and how often does it produce unsupported claims?
- **RQ2.** Can the platform correctly identify intelligence that is, or is not, suitable for detection engineering?
- **RQ3.** What proportion of generated rules are structurally valid on the first attempt and after bounded automated repair?
- **RQ4.** How accurately does the platform map behaviours to MITRE ATT&CK techniques?
- **RQ5.** Do the hard gates prevent seeded defective rules and prompt-injection attempts from reaching approval or deployment?
- **RQ6.** How does rule quality, judged blind by independent raters and measured on held-out events, compare between manual, ATIDEP and single-prompt conditions?
- **RQ7.** Does feedback-based tuning reduce false positives on benign events while retaining true-positive detections?

Two further questions concern the economic dimension. They are separated from RQ1 to RQ7 because their answers rest on a cost model with sourced inputs and a pre-registered decision rule, not on a significance test of sampled outcomes.

- **RQ8.** What does the workflow cost, in physical quantities and in money, per report processed, per detection generated, per detection that passes every hard gate, per detection approved and per detection deployed, under named monthly workloads, how does that compare with the manual workflow, and how much of each figure is measured, taken from a cited source, assumed or still missing?
- **RQ9.** When the workflow is technically adequate, is it substantially cheaper than a modelled paid configuration of the same functions under the pre-registered decision rule, and which assumptions would reverse that conclusion?

## Research approach

The thesis follows a design-science approach: it builds an artefact, ATIDEP, to address an identified problem, and evaluates the artefact rigorously [@hevner2004design]. The process follows the usual sequence of problem identification, definition of objectives, design and development, demonstration, evaluation and communication [@peffers2007dsrm].

The evaluation is a pre-registered experiment on 30 intelligence items, plus a separate pilot set used only for tuning. Three conditions are compared: a manual workflow, the full ATIDEP workflow with human review, and a single-prompt LLM baseline that receives the same information but no pipeline; ablations remove the validators and the repair loop. Two primary hypotheses concern efficiency (H1: analyst active time per item is reduced by at least a pilot-fixed margin) and quality (H4: rules approved through ATIDEP are not worse than manual rules by more than a pilot-fixed margin). Quality is tested as *non-inferiority*, since a non-significant difference does not demonstrate equivalence [@schuirmann1987tost; @lakens2017equivalence]. Secondary hypotheses concern structural validity (H2), the effectiveness of the gates against seeded defects and injected instructions (H3), the contribution of the pipeline relative to a single prompt (H5) and the effect of feedback-based tuning (H6). The design counters learning and carry-over effects by counterbalancing the order of conditions with a washout period, estimates the effect of order explicitly, uses blinded raters other than the researcher, and controls for training-data contamination by using reports published after the model's training cutoff, since benchmark exposure during training can inflate results [@sainz2023contamination]. Economic cost-effectiveness is assessed on its own dimension (Chapter 8): a cost model compares the manual workflow, the workflow as built and a modelled paid configuration built from the same functions by component substitution, whose technical performance is assumed equal and never measured; a pre-registered decision rule (H7) states what "substantially cheaper" means; descriptive analyses give normalised unit costs, break-even volumes and sensitivity; and a joint assessment reads technical adequacy and cost together in wording fixed in advance. The full protocol, hypotheses and statistical plan are given in Chapter 3.

## Scope and delimitations

The study is deliberately bounded. ATIDEP is a laboratory prototype, not a production system, and it is not a SIEM or an autonomous response platform: it connects intelligence sources to portable detection content and a controlled target. The target is a manager-only instance of the stable Wazuh 4.14 line, a free and open-source platform that unifies XDR and SIEM capabilities [@wazuh2026overview]. The input types are limited to feeds, uploaded files and allowlisted URLs. The converter handles a documented subset of Sigma, and constructs outside the subset are rejected. The organisation is synthetic, a single language model is pinned for the experiment, and the dataset has 30 test items. Production deployment, real malware execution, customer data, STIX/TAXII ingestion, and MISP or OpenCTI integration are out of scope. No commercial product is run, installed or benchmarked. The paid configuration is a modelled counterfactual priced only from published list prices or written quotations that can be cited, with unpriced items reported as unpriced; the thesis does not claim that ATIDEP is equivalent to any paid system, and it does not invent a price, a labour rate or a workload volume.

## Contributions

The thesis makes the following contributions. It does not claim to advance the state of the art in the raw quality of LLM-generated detection rules, which is the focus of the generation systems discussed in Chapter 2, and it does not claim that any single component is new: evidence-located extraction, governance of extracted claims, validity gates, refusing converters and validator-driven repair all have precedents (Section 2.16). Its contributions are in integrating these into one governed workflow, in conversion to an open-source target, and in evaluation. Each is stated as a design or a method; whether it achieves its purpose is decided by the results in Chapters 7 and 8. If the pending full-text reading of the closest works (Table 2.10) shows that one of them already provides a contribution listed here, the item will be narrowed accordingly.

1. A reference design for CTI-to-detection automation in which deterministic gates and tests surround bounded LLM steps, with every consequential action fail-closed and gated by a hash-bound human approval.
2. An evidence-verification method in which every extracted claim carries a verbatim quotation that is checked by substring match against the sanitised source.
3. A documented Wazuh-compatible subset of Sigma and a conservative converter that refuses what it cannot translate faithfully, with a measurement of conversion fidelity between a Sigma-level matcher and Wazuh's own rule tester.
4. A bounded repair loop driven by deterministic validators, with first-pass and post-repair validity reported separately.
5. A pre-registered, bias-controlled comparison of manual, governed-pipeline and single-prompt workflows, using a blinded rubric, held-out events, and non-inferiority testing.
6. Seeded-defect and prompt-injection test sets for evaluating the controls of a detection-engineering pipeline.
7. A pre-registered economic evaluation of the open-source workflow against a manual workflow and a functionally comparable modelled paid configuration: a cost model with explicit perimeters and ledger categories, a sourced price register that never invents a price, normalised unit costs, a decision rule that handles incomplete totals honestly, sensitivity and tipping points, a hidden-cost register, an audit of the open-source claim including the status of the language model, and a joint assessment of technical adequacy and cost whose wording cannot overclaim.

## Structure of the thesis

Chapter 2, the literature review and state of the art, reviews by theme the work on threat-intelligence quality, extraction of attack behaviour from reports, generation of detection rules, conversion to Wazuh, validation and quality of detection content, language models in security operations and their reliability and attack surface, human oversight, deployment and lifecycle, the meaning of "open source" for a pipeline that contains a model, and the economics of such automation; it compares the closest works in a matrix, states the limitations of existing approaches and the research gap, and traces each challenge of this chapter to the component and the experimental condition that address it. Chapter 3 presents the methodology, including hypotheses, experimental design, metrics and statistical analysis. Chapter 4 sets out requirements and architecture, and Chapter 5 describes the implementation. Chapter 6 describes the experimental setup, and Chapter 7 reports the technical results. Chapter 8 presents the economic evaluation and the joint assessment of technical adequacy and cost. Chapter 9 discusses the findings, Chapter 10 examines threats to validity, and Chapter 11 concludes with answers to the research questions, limitations and future work.
